## Historical Context

"Didn't It Rain" retells the story of Noah and the Flood (Genesis 6–9) — the ark built against the coming judgment, the animals gathered two by two, the forty days and nights of rain, and the eventual sign of the raven and the dove. The song appeared in published form in a 1919 arrangement for voice and piano by the composer Harry T. Burleigh, and it was documented among the spirituals collected by James Weldon Johnson and John Wesley Work. It was later popularized by gospel artists including Sister Rosetta Tharpe and Mahalia Jackson in the mid-twentieth century.

## Cultural Significance

The Flood narrative held a double meaning for the enslaved community, as the great stories of the Hebrew Bible so often did. On one hand, it was a story of destruction — the drowning of a whole wicked world. On the other, it was a story of deliverance: the faithful remnant, Noah and his family, carried safely through the catastrophe in the ark God told them to build. For a people who understood themselves as a faithful remnant within a corrupt and violent order, the promise that God would carry His own through the deluge — and judge the world that oppressed them — was potent.

Yet the song's tone is not grim. "Didn't It Rain" is sung as a jubilee, a rollicking, celebratory shout, full of the homely detail of an ark built "out of hickory bark" and the comic parade of animals "two by two." This transformation of a judgment narrative into joyful celebration is characteristic of the tradition, which could hold awe and delight together, rehearsing God's terrible power with something close to glee — because that power was, finally, on the side of the oppressed.

## Scholarly Notes

Harry T. Burleigh's 1919 published arrangement is an important document in the song's history and in the broader movement, led by Burleigh and others, to establish the spiritual as concert art. (As with other published arrangements, Burleigh's specific setting had its own copyright; this entry documents the traditional song.)

James Weldon Johnson and John Wesley Work documented the Noah songs within the tradition's deep engagement with the narratives of the Hebrew Bible, read typologically as stories of the faithful delivered and the wicked judged.

Mahalia Jackson's and Sister Rosetta Tharpe's recordings carried "Didn't It Rain" into the gospel mainstream, where its driving energy and vivid storytelling made it a showpiece, and it remains a staple of the jubilee and gospel repertoires.
