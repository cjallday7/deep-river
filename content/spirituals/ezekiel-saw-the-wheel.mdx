## Historical Context

"Ezekiel Saw the Wheel" draws directly on the opening vision of the Book of Ezekiel, in which the prophet, exiled in Babylon, beholds a whirlwind, a great cloud, and "a wheel in the middle of a wheel" moving with the four living creatures (Ezekiel 1:16). This strange and majestic image — a vision of the mobility and omnipresence of God's glory even in a land of captivity — held obvious resonance for a people held in bondage.

The song was among the spirituals collected and carried into public performance by the Fisk Jubilee Singers in the 1870s and documented in J.B.T. Marsh's account of their work. It was later anthologized by James Weldon Johnson and preserved in the Hampton Institute tradition through R. Nathaniel Dett's *Religious Folk-Songs of the Negro* (1927).

## Cultural Significance

The genius of the spiritual is the way it moves between registers. The refrain and opening verse render Ezekiel's cosmic vision as awe and praise — "the big wheel run by faith, the little wheel run by the grace of God." But the later verses turn abruptly and wittily toward the moral life of the community: warnings against the fair-weather convert who is "turned out" within six months, and against the "hypocrite" who gossips.

This movement from the sublime to the pointed and satirical is characteristic of the tradition. The same song that contemplates the machinery of heaven also holds the congregation accountable to one another, using humor and plain speech to police the boundaries of sincere faith. The "wheel in a wheel" became, in later interpretation, an emblem of interlocking human and divine purpose.

## Scholarly Notes

James Weldon Johnson observed that spirituals frequently fused theological imagery with sharp social commentary in a single song, and "Ezekiel Saw the Wheel" is a clear example: the hypocrite verses circulated widely and appear, in variant forms, attached to several different spirituals.

R. Nathaniel Dett, the composer and Hampton director who edited *Religious Folk-Songs of the Negro* (1927), worked to preserve such songs in performing editions while insisting on the integrity of their folk origins. His arrangements helped carry the spiritual into the twentieth-century choral repertoire.

The song's vivid central image has had a long afterlife in African American culture and beyond, and its melody and refrain remain among the most widely recognized in the spiritual tradition.
