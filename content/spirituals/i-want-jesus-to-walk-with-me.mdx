## Historical Context

"I Want Jesus to Walk with Me" is a prayer song of the antebellum tradition, carried in oral tradition among enslaved African Americans and later preserved through the Fisk Jubilee Singers' repertoire and the collecting work of John Wesley Work and James Weldon Johnson. Its language is spare and direct, without narrative or biblical spectacle — a single, repeated petition.

The song belongs to the intimate strand of the spirituals: not the great communal shouts of jubilee or judgment, but the quiet, first-person prayers that express the inner life of the believer. Its subject is companionship — the longing not to be alone in suffering.

## Cultural Significance

The theology at the heart of the song is the theology of Emmanuel, "God with us" — the conviction that Jesus is not a distant sovereign but a companion who walks beside the sufferer. For an enslaved people, whose isolation was one of the deliberate cruelties of the system, the promise of a presence that could not be sold away, separated, or forbidden was profound.

The image of the "pilgrim journey" frames life itself as a long walk through hardship toward a destination beyond it. Each verse names a trial — trials, sorrows, a heart "almost breaking" — and answers it not with the removal of suffering but with the presence of a companion within it. This is the tradition's characteristic realism: it does not pretend the road is easy, only that the traveler need not walk it alone.

The song has remained central to African American worship and was a source of strength in the Civil Rights Movement, where the request for Jesus to "walk with me" spoke directly to those who literally walked and marched in the face of danger.

## Scholarly Notes

James Weldon Johnson, in *The Second Book of Negro Spirituals* (1926), grouped the prayer songs as a distinct and essential part of the tradition — songs whose power lies in their restraint and sincerity rather than in dramatic imagery.

Howard Thurman's meditations on the spirituals emphasized their refusal to deny suffering while insisting on the nearness of God within it. "I Want Jesus to Walk with Me" is a clear statement of that theology, holding hardship and hope together in a single line.

The song has been interpreted by many of the great spiritual and concert artists, including Marian Anderson, whose recordings brought its quiet intensity to wide audiences, and it remains a staple of both the sanctuary and the recital hall.
