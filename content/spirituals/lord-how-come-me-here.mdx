## Historical Context

"Lord, How Come Me Here?" is among the most devastating of all the sorrow songs — a spiritual that does not console, does not promise deliverance, and does not look toward heaven, but simply asks God an unanswerable question and states an unbearable wish. It was documented in the Hampton Institute's *Cabin and Plantation Songs* (1874), preserved in R. Nathaniel Dett's later Hampton edition, and noted among the spirituals by James Weldon Johnson.

Unlike most spirituals, which move from grief toward hope, this one stays in the grief. Its refrain — "I wish I never was born" — is a lament as raw as any in scripture, kin to the cries of Job and Jeremiah, who each cursed the day of their birth.

## Cultural Significance

The song is a rare and precious thing: an undisguised protest, sung directly to God, naming the evils of slavery without code or consolation. "There ain't no freedom here." "They treat me so mean here." "They sold my children away." These are not veiled or typological statements; they are plain testimony to the specific cruelties of bondage — the absence of liberty, the daily brutality, and the deepest wound of all, the sale of one's children.

That the tradition contained such a song is essential to understanding it. The spirituals were not only music of hope, faith, and coded resistance; they were also, sometimes, the unvarnished cry of anguish, the refusal to pretend that suffering was bearable. "Lord, how come me here?" asks the question that slavery made unavoidable — why am I in this?, how did this come to be? — and declines to supply a comforting answer.

To address the question to God is itself an act of faith of a severe kind: it assumes God is there to be asked, and holds God accountable to hear. The lament is a form of relationship, even when it is a relationship of protest. In singing this song, a community refused to let its suffering go unspoken before God.

## Scholarly Notes

The song's preservation in the 1874 Hampton collection is significant precisely because its content is so unsparing; it documents that the enslaved community produced and sang songs of open grievance, not only songs of hope.

R. Nathaniel Dett, in editing the Hampton spirituals, valued such songs for their emotional honesty and resisted the sentimentalizing tendency that softened the tradition for concert audiences.

Scholars of the spirituals, from W.E.B. Du Bois forward, have pointed to laments like this one as evidence that the sorrow songs confronted suffering directly, without denial. Twentieth-century artists including Kathleen Battle have performed "Lord, How Come Me Here?" as a stark, unresolved lament — a reminder that the tradition's hope was earned against the full weight of despair, not in ignorance of it.
