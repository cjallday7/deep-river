## Historical Context

"Lord, I Want to Be a Christian" is among the gentlest and most inward of the spirituals — a prayer song without narrative or drama, structured as a series of humble petitions. It was documented in the Hampton collection *Cabin and Plantation Songs* (1874) and preserved in R. Nathaniel Dett's later Hampton editions.

Tradition connects the song's origins to the mid-eighteenth-century evangelical awakening in Virginia, and to Presbyterian missionary work among enslaved people in the region of Hanover County. According to accounts passed down in the Hampton tradition, the song arose from an enslaved man's expressed longing to become a Christian. This origin story cannot be documented with the certainty of a written source and should be treated as tradition rather than established fact — but it places the song among the oldest strata of the repertoire.

## Cultural Significance

The refrain of every verse — "in my heart" — is the song's whole theology. The petitions are not for outward circumstance but for inward transformation: to be more loving, more holy, more like Jesus. In a world that denied enslaved people control over nearly every external condition of their lives, the heart remained a domain that could not be seized. The song claims that inner ground as the true site of religious life.

This inwardness carried its own quiet power. To sing "I want to be a Christian in my heart" was to assert that genuine faith is a matter of the soul's disposition, not of the enslaver's permission or the world's recognition. It located dignity and moral agency in a place beyond the reach of the auction block.

## Scholarly Notes

R. Nathaniel Dett and the Hampton editors regarded this song as one of the tradition's purest examples of the "prayer song" — a form marked by simplicity, repetition, and the absence of the dramatic biblical imagery found in the shout songs and jubilees.

John Wesley Work documented the song within the broader body of spirituals concerned with personal holiness and conversion, a strand of the tradition sometimes overshadowed in later scholarship by the more overtly political songs, but central to the lived religion of the communities who sang them.

The song entered mainstream American hymnody in the twentieth century and appears in the hymnals of many denominations, one of the spirituals most fully absorbed into common Protestant worship.
