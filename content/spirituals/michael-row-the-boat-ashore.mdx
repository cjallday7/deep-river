## Historical Context

"Michael Row the Boat Ashore" is a spiritual of the Gullah people of the Sea Islands off the coast of South Carolina and Georgia. It was first written down during the Civil War at St. Helena Island, sung by formerly enslaved people whose enslavers had fled before the arrival of the Union fleet. Charles Pickard Ware, a Harvard-educated abolitionist who supervised plantations on St. Helena from 1862 to 1865, transcribed the song as he heard the freedpeople sing it.

It was published in 1867 in *Slave Songs of the United States* — the first substantial published collection of African American spirituals, compiled by William Francis Allen, Charles Pickard Ware, and Lucy McKim Garrison. That collection remains one of the most important primary documents of the tradition, preserving songs and dialect from the Sea Islands at the very moment of emancipation.

## Cultural Significance

The "Michael" of the song is the archangel Michael, who in Christian tradition ferries souls across the river of death — here fused with the Jordan, the boundary of the Promised Land. To "row the boat ashore" is to complete the crossing, to bring the soul safely over. The instruction to "sister, help to trim the sail" makes the crossing a shared labor: no one rows alone.

But the song was also, literally, a rowing song. The people of the Sea Islands moved through a landscape of tidal creeks and open water, and songs like this set the rhythm of the oars. In that setting the spiritual carried a layered meaning at once sacred and immediate: the Jordan of the soul and the salt water of the low country, the crossing into heaven and the daily passage of a people learning to move freely across waters they had once crossed only at another's command.

The verse "chills the body but not the soul" states the tradition's central conviction — that the deepest self, the soul, lies beyond the reach of every cold and every chain.

## Scholarly Notes

*Slave Songs of the United States* (1867) is the foundational primary source for the Sea Island spirituals, and its editors were unusually attentive to the difficulty of notating music that depended on inflection, glide, and communal improvisation. Their prefatory notes are among the earliest serious attempts to describe the character of African American sacred song.

Scholars of Gullah culture, including those who have studied the Penn Center on St. Helena Island, have documented how the Sea Islands' relative isolation preserved older forms of African American language, music, and religious practice. "Michael Row the Boat Ashore" is a product of that world.

The song reached a mass audience in 1960 through a recording by the folk group The Highwaymen, which reached number one — a popularization that, like many, carried the melody far from its origins on St. Helena Island. This entry documents the traditional Sea Island spiritual as it was first collected in 1867.
