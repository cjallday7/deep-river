## Historical Context

"Rock-a My Soul" takes its central image from the Gospel of Luke's parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31), in which the poor beggar Lazarus, after death, is carried by angels "into Abraham's bosom" — a place of comfort and honor — while the rich man suffers. For a people who lived in poverty and deprivation, the promise that the humble would be cradled in Abraham's embrace was a vision of ultimate belonging and rest.

The song was carried by the Fisk Jubilee Singers and preserved in the collecting work of John Wesley Work and James Weldon Johnson. Its verse "so high you can't get over it, so low you can't get under it, so wide you can't get around it" circulated widely and attached itself to several spirituals, describing the vastness and inescapability of God's love or of heaven itself.

## Cultural Significance

The word "rock" gives the song its tenderness. To be rocked is to be soothed as a child is soothed — held, comforted, protected. In a tradition full of thunder and judgment, "Rock-a My Soul" is gentle: it imagines the afterlife not as a courtroom but as an embrace. That the embrace belongs to Lazarus, the beggar, and not to the rich man, reinforces the tradition's persistent theology of reversal, in which the poor are lifted and the mighty brought low.

The valley verse — "When I went down in the valley to pray, my soul got happy and I stayed all day" — captures the ecstatic experience of worship, the joy that could overtake a gathering and hold it. The whole song moves between rest and rejoicing, between the cradling comfort of the refrain and the exuberance of the verses.

## Scholarly Notes

James Weldon Johnson, in *The Second Book of Negro Spirituals* (1926), included this song among the joyful spirituals whose buoyancy contrasted with the sorrow songs — evidence, he argued, of the full emotional range of the tradition, which held grief and gladness together.

John Wesley Work documented the migratory "so high / so low / so wide" verse and other floating stanzas, noting how such interchangeable units of song moved freely across the repertoire, recombined by singers according to the needs of the moment.

The song's infectious rhythm and structure — the refrain and verse can be sung simultaneously as interlocking parts — have made it a durable favorite in choral, camp, and congregational settings, though this popular use has sometimes obscured the depth of the biblical vision at its heart.
