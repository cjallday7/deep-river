## Historical Context

"Walk Together, Children" is a communal marching and exhortation song, calling the whole gathered community to move forward as one. Its promised destination — "a great camp meeting in the promised land" — draws on the camp meeting, the central institution of African American religious life: the large outdoor gathering for preaching, singing, and worship that served as a rare space of collective assembly and joy.

The song was carried by the Fisk Jubilee Singers, documented in the Fisk repertoire, and preserved in the collecting work of John Wesley Work and James Weldon Johnson, who included it among the spirituals in his second anthology.

## Cultural Significance

The imperative at the song's heart is collective: *walk together*. The journey it imagines is not made alone but by a whole people moving in company, and the encouragement — "don't you get weary" — is directed to the community as a body. In a system designed to isolate and divide enslaved people, the insistence on walking *together* was itself an act of solidarity and resistance.

The verses build a vision of tireless devotion — to mourn, to sing, to pray, and "never tire." This is endurance transfigured into worship: the very acts that sustain a suffering community are also the acts that will fill the great camp meeting at journey's end. The "promised land" is at once heaven and, in the layered meaning of the tradition, the land of freedom.

The song's message of solidarity and perseverance made it a natural resource for the twentieth-century freedom struggle, where its language of walking together and not growing weary spoke directly to the discipline of collective, sustained nonviolent action.

## Scholarly Notes

James Weldon Johnson, in *The Second Book of Negro Spirituals* (1926), grouped this among the songs whose primary function was communal encouragement — songs that bound a gathering together and carried it through the long labor of worship and struggle alike.

John Wesley Work documented the centrality of the camp meeting to the religious world that produced the spirituals, and songs like this one make that world audible: they were composed for and by people singing in company, in motion, sustaining one another.

The phrase "don't you get weary" and the theme of walking together have echoed through African American sacred and freedom music for more than a century, marking this song as one of the tradition's enduring statements of collective hope.
